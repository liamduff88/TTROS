--- Q3/25 [A3] How is delivery actually done?
    latency_s=24.1 manifest=/home/liam/agentic-os-live/queue/context_assemblies/hermes-20260907_134517_e455bb-20260907_134517_e455bb:d1e2b15b-e30e-463d-87cd-86ceb8db90b8:681ee65d.json session_id=20260907_134517_e455bb
    ANSWER: Delivery is done as a forward-deployed build cycle, not as advice.

The real shape is:

1. Scope the client and isolate the work
   - Need client entity page, signed scope, and a client-isolation folder.
   - If those are missing, delivery should not start.
   - This prevents “helpful chaos” and protects client data boundaries.

2. Pick one workflow
   - Delivery starts around one operational workflow, not “the business.”
   - Examples:
     - speed-to-lead
     - client memory / knowledge base
     - AI operations support
     - quick-win scan
   - If the notes span multiple workflows, that is a scope problem, not a reason to improvise.

3. Map the current state
   - Where work enters.
   - Who touches it.
   - Where it waits.
   - What falls through.
   - What systems are involved.
   - What numbers or timestamps prove the bottleneck.

4. Diagnose one bottleneck
   - The diagnosis should be tied to revenue, delivery capacity, lead response, leakage, or operational drag.
   - For a Quick-Win Scan: one bottleneck only.
   - For a build: the bottleneck becomes the workflow design target.

5. Design the target workflow
   - Capture → route → respond → follow up.
   - Or source → consolidate → cite → retrieve.
   - Or session → actions → decisions → next build.
   - Every human approval gate is marked.
   - Client sign-off is required before implementation.

6. Build locally or inside the client stack
   - Scripts, automations, CRM configuration, knowledge base structure, dashboards, docs, or operating procedures.
   - Read/draft-only unless there is explicit approval for external mutation.
   - No auto-sending to real leads unless separately approved.

7. Test with evidence
   - Not “looks good.”
   - For speed-to-lead: test leads must traverse every scoped inbound path within target response time.
   - For client memory: sample claims must trace back to source links, and real questions must be answerable from the base.
   - For ops support: hub updates, decisions, actions, and month summary must exist on time.

8. Handoff
   - Client gets:
     - what the system does
     - what it will not do
     - how to pause it
     - how to maintain it
     - who owns what
   - This matters because TTR is selling working operating capability, not dependency theatre.

9. Closeout
   - Update entity page.
   - Write receipt.
   - Record evidence.
   - Revoke or close out access where appropriate.
   - Preserve token / delivery record internally.

The honest answer: the delivery model is clearer than the delivery proof.

Verified from the context: TTR has defined delivery playbooks for Quick-Win Scan, Speed-to-Lead, AI Operations Support, and Client Memory. The strongest common operating pattern is map → design → build → test → handoff → close, with evidence and approval gates.

Interpretation: the core offer is credible if Liam actually runs it on a narrow client workflow and produces evidence. It becomes weak if it stays as architecture, queue process, or “agent operating system” explanation.

My recommendation: sell and deliver the smallest paid version first: a CA$350 Quick-Win Scan or one bounded Speed-to-Lead build. The point is not to perfect the delivery system. The point is to create two implementation proofs.

Cheapest test: take one real prospect/client workflow and ask, “Can we map one revenue leak, prescribe one fix, and produce a useful artifact in 48 hours without touching external systems?” If yes, delivery is real. If not, the offer is still mostly internal theory.

Specifically, to a prospect, do not claim:

- Case studies that do not exist.
- Credentials, partnerships, certifications, or industry authority we have not earned.
- Vertical expertise beyond what TTR has actually delivered.
- That TTR has a forward-deployed engineering or GTM engineering track record beyond what has actually been built.
- That Agentic OS / TTROS is a product, SaaS, platform, or thing sold to clients.

- Prospect-specific facts we have not verified: urgency, budget, tech stack, buying intent, pain, internal workflows, or current priorities.
- Shared history, familiarity, referrals, or “I saw you were struggling with…” unless there is a real public signal.
- Compliance-sensitive pretexts: misleading subject lines, fake research, fake community connection, false urgency.
- ROI promises. We can discuss possible levers; we cannot promise numbers.

What we can claim:

- TTR finds and removes operational friction in growing service businesses.
- TTR builds revenue, data, and operations systems against the client’s real workflows.
- TTR works as a two-discipline engineering practice: forward-deployed engineering and GTM engineering.
- TTR is currently solo-founder, pre-vertical, and building proof through implementation work.
- TTR’s own acquisition engine is being built as a proof of the same class of system it sells.
- Any sourced public observation about the prospect, clearly framed as an observation.
- Hypotheses, if labelled as hypotheses: “My guess is there may be leakage around lead response or handoff — worth checking.”

The safe prospecting posture is:

“I noticed X public signal. My hypothesis is Y might be creating friction. I help service businesses find and remove that kind of revenue/ops bottleneck by building against the real workflow, not writing recommendations. Worth a short fit call?”

No fake proof. No invented authority. No ROI promise. Use specificity from the prospect, not inflated claims about us.

--- Q7/25 [B2] When would we use "forward-deployed engineering" language, and when not?

    ANSWER: Use “forward-deployed engineering” when you need to signal how TTR works to a buyer who will value embedded, practical, technical execution.

Do not use it as the first-line public pitch to a normal service-business owner.

My read:

Use it when the audience already understands the pain of generic consultants, agencies, or software vendors.

Good places to use it:
- Website “how we work” section, after the plain-English problem/offer.
- Proposals or sales calls with operators, founders, COOs, RevOps leaders, technical-adjacent buyers.
- Explaining why TTR is not a strategy deck, automation shop, or SaaS tool.
- Positioning against consultants: “We work as forward-deployed engineers: inside the live operation, against real workflows and numbers, until the system works.”
- Hiring/collaborator language later, if TTR needs to attract people who know that operating style.
- Internal doctrine: it is a useful discipline name for what TTR must actually do.

Use plainer language when the buyer is not likely to know or care what “forward-deployed” means.

Better first-line buyer language:
- “We embed inside the operation and fix the workflows that slow revenue down.”
- “We build the systems your team actually uses, against your real data and daily work.”
- “Not recommendations — working operating systems.”
- “We find where revenue is leaking, then design and build the workflow, data, and automation fixes.”

Avoid “forward-deployed engineering” when:
- Cold outreach subject lines or first messages need instant clarity.
- The buyer is a local service-business owner, sales manager, or non-technical operator.
- You are trying to reduce perceived complexity.
- It sounds like Palantir cosplay or enterprise jargon.
- You cannot yet back it with implementation proof.
- The phrase becomes a way to admire the model instead of selling the result.

The rule I’d use:

Public headline: no.
Offer page body: yes, but translated immediately.
Sales conversation: yes, if the buyer is sophisticated enough.
Internal operating model: yes.
Cold outbound: mostly no.

Example hierarchy:

Headline:
“We fix the operational friction slowing revenue in growing service businesses.”

Subhead:
“TTR embeds inside your real workflows, finds the bottlenecks, and builds the revenue, data, and operations systems needed to remove them.”

Method section:
“This is forward-deployed engineering: delivery in the client’s actual operating context, against live workflows and real numbers — not detached recommendations.”

The phrase is valuable, but it should be a precision tool, not the storefront sign. The storefront sign should be the pain and result.
