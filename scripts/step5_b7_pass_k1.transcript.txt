--- Q3/25 [A3] How is delivery actually done?
    latency_s=23.2 manifest=/home/liam/agentic-os-live/queue/context_assemblies/hermes-20260907_102928_08128f-20260907_102928_08128f:de55684d-624c-49a1-a5e1-55806134709b:2b9104be.json
    ANSWER: Delivery is done as embedded engineering, not consulting theatre.

The practical shape is:

1. Start with a real client workflow
Not “improve operations” in the abstract. Pick one workflow that affects revenue, response time, delivery quality, admin load, or decision-making.

Examples:
- inbound lead capture and follow-up
- client onboarding
- quote/proposal flow
- handoff from sales to delivery
- scattered client/team knowledge
- recurring ops/admin process

2. Get the required inputs before work starts
For any paid delivery, TTR needs:
- client entity/context page
- signed scope or agreed delivery contract
- client-isolated working folder
- the actual source material: notes, workflows, systems, recordings, forms, CRM exports, inbox examples, SOPs, etc.

If those are missing, delivery should stop. Otherwise we drift into unpaid diagnosis or fake certainty.

3. Map the current reality
TTR traces how the workflow actually works today:
- where information enters
- who touches it
- where it waits
- what gets copied manually
- what gets lost
- where the client/customer feels delay
- what numbers exist
- what systems are involved
- what approval gates are required

This is forward-deployed: build against the client’s messy operation, not an idealized diagram.

4. Diagnose the bottleneck
The output is not a huge transformation plan. It is usually:
- the one highest-leverage bottleneck
- why it matters commercially
- what proof exists
- what assumptions are being made
- what the fix should be

For a Quick-Win Scan, this becomes a short report: current state → one bottleneck → why it matters → one fix → first step → soft upgrade path.

5. Design the target workflow
Before building, TTR defines:
- what the workflow should do
- what humans still approve
- what the system can draft or automate
- what data/source rules apply
- what acceptance test proves it works
- what is explicitly out of scope

For builds like Speed-to-Lead or Client Memory, client sign-off is required before implementation.

6. Build inside scope
Implementation can include:
- CRM/workflow configuration
- forms and routing
- automations
- dashboards
- AI-assisted drafting
- knowledge bases
- scripts
- SOPs
- handoff docs
- internal tools

But the rule is: read/draft/local work is ungated; external sends, CRM mutations, publishing, credentials, money, or client commitments require approval.

7. Test with real acceptance evidence
“Done” means the workflow was exercised.

Examples:
- a test lead traverses every scoped inbound path within the target response time
- a knowledge base answers a defined set of real business questions with source-backed answers
- a client hub is updated within 24 hours after a session
- a scan has exactly one workflow, one bottleneck, one next step, and labelled assumptions

No “looks good” passes.

8. Handoff and close
TTR gives the client:
- what was built
- how it works
- how to pause it
- what it will not do without approval
- who owns updates
- what evidence proves acceptance
- what the next recommended build is, if there is one

Then TTR writes the receipt, updates the client record, and closes access where appropriate.

The commercial model underneath this is simple:

- Quick-Win Scan: diagnose one workflow and produce a small, useful next action.
- Build: implement one scoped system or workflow.
- Retainer: keep improving operations monthly through working sessions, follow-up, hub updates, and recommended next builds.

My interpretation: delivery should be sold as “we embed, map one revenue-relevant workflow, remove the bottleneck, and prove the new workflow works.” Not “AI transformation,” not “ops consulting,” and not “we’ll build whatever you need.”

--- Q4/25 [A4] What are we not allowed to claim to a prospect?

    ANSWER: We are not allowed to claim anything we cannot evidence.

Specifically, to a prospect we should not claim:

1. Client results we do not have
   - No “we increased revenue by X”
   - No “we saved teams Y hours”
   - No implied case studies unless there is a sourced client result.

2. Revenue figures or ROI promises
   - No guaranteed ROI.
   - No projected ROI framed as achieved.
   - No “this will pay for itself in 30 days” unless proven for that client context, which we do not have.

3. Case studies that do not exist
   - No composite stories.
   - No anonymized “client example” unless it is a real sourced outcome and properly anonymized.

4. Credentials or track record TTR has not earned
   - No vertical expertise claims.
   - No “we specialize in your industry” unless actually true.
   - No forward-deployed / GTM engineering track record beyond what TTR has actually built.

5. Partnerships, affiliations, or access we do not have
   - No implying we are partnered with platforms, CRMs, AI vendors, or client companies unless evidenced.

6. Proprietary methods that are not real
   - We can describe the working approach.
   - We should not claim a named proprietary system, secret method, or proven framework unless it exists and has evidence behind it.

7. That Agentic OS is a product
   - Agentic OS / TTROS is internal infrastructure.
   - It is not SaaS.
   - It is not sold to clients.
   - The client-facing offer is TTR solving operational and revenue-system friction.

8. Prospect-specific facts we have not verified
   - No invented tech stack.
   - No invented pain.
   - No assumed budget.
   - No “I noticed your team is struggling with…” unless tied to an observable public signal and framed as a hypothesis.

9. False familiarity or false pretext
   - No pretending to know them.
   - No invented mutual context.
   - No “following your work for a while” unless true.
   - No fake urgency or fake interest signals.

10. Compliance-sensitive claims
   - No misleading subject lines or commercial-message disguises.
   - No platform action, email send, LinkedIn send, CRM mutation, or calendar booking without approval.

The safe posture is:

“I help growing service businesses find and remove operational friction in their revenue and delivery systems. I’m building this from direct implementation work, not selling a SaaS. From the outside, I noticed [specific public signal]. My hypothesis is [possible bottleneck]. Worth a short conversation to see if that is real?”

That keeps us honest: observable signal, labelled hypothesis, low-friction next step, no fabricated proof.
